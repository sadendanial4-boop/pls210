Political Science Research Methods — Proposal Part I

Does income inequality reduce voter turnout among low-income citizens in U.S. states, 2000–2020?

Positivist U.S. state-level comparison 5 presidential election cycles

Research Question

  • Empirical: asks about a measurable relationship, not what ought to happen
  • Population: low-income citizens (bottom income quintile)
  • Context: U.S. states as units of comparison
  • Time: five presidential elections, 2000–2020
  • Answerable: Gini coefficients from the American Community Survey; turnout by income from the Current Population Survey Voting Supplement — both free and public

Epistemological Positioning — Positivist

  • Social reality exists independently of the researcher and can be measured
  • Knowledge comes from testing falsifiable hypotheses against data
  • Goal: identify generalisable patterns across states and elections
  • Fits this question because both variables — inequality and turnout — are quantifiable and consistently measured across all 50 states
  • Interpretivist would be needed only if the question asked how citizens experience their decision not to vote — this one does not

Contribution

  • Most studies measure aggregate turnout — all income groups combined — which masks who is actually being demobilised
  • This study isolates the bottom income quintile for a more precise test
  • State-level panel across five elections allows fixed-effects controls for time-stable state factors
  • Core contribution: methodological precision on a well-theorised but bluntly-measured relationship

Literature Review

  • 1

    Verba, Schlozman & Brady — Voice and Equality (1995)

    Civic Voluntarism Model: participation depends on time, money, and civic skills. Low-income citizens are disadvantaged on all three. → provides the causal mechanism.

  • 2

    Solt — "Economic Inequality and Democratic Political Engagement" (2008)

    Cross-national evidence: higher inequality → lower voting among the non-wealthy. Demobilising effect strongest at the bottom of the income distribution. → closest empirical predecessor.

  • 3

    Leighley & Nagler — Who Votes Now? (2014)

    Documents the persistent income–turnout gap in U.S. elections 1972–2008. The gap has not narrowed despite rising education. → justifies studying 2000–2020.

  • 4

    Lijphart — "Unequal Participation" (1997)

    Low-income non-voting as a democratic problem: less participation → policy favours the wealthy → deeper inequality → less participation. → frames the normative stakes.

  • 5

    Meltzer & Richard — "A Rational Theory of the Size of Government" (1981)

    Classical model: higher inequality should increase redistribution demand among median voters, mobilising the poor. Used here as a foil — the observed demobilisation is the puzzle.

Theory and Hypotheses

H1: U.S. states with higher income inequality (Gini coefficient) will show lower voter turnout among citizens in the bottom income quintile in presidential elections, controlling for state electoral laws, racial composition, and urbanisation.

Independent Variable

State-level Gini coefficient
U.S. Census / ACS — per election year

Causal Mechanism

Resource deprivation — inequality reduces time, money & civic skills, raising the cost of participation for low-income citizens

Dependent Variable

Turnout rate, bottom income quintile
Current Population Survey Voting Supplement

  • Framework: Civic Voluntarism Model (Verba et al. 1995) — participation = f(time, money, civic skills)
  • Falsifiable: no significant effect after controls → mechanism not supported

What Working with AI Helped Me Understand — and What I Still Don't

Ungraded — shows where my thinking is

What became clearer

  • Why IV/DV precision matters — "inequality affects voting" is too vague; the mechanism must be specified
  • Why Meltzer-Richard makes the puzzle interesting — the poor should be mobilised, so demobilisation needs explaining
  • Why positivism fits — the question is about aggregate measurable behaviour, not individual interpretation

What I still find uncertain

  • How to run and interpret a fixed-effects model in practice
  • How to handle reverse causality — does inequality suppress turnout, or does low turnout enable inequality-deepening policy?
  • What instrumental variable approaches are and whether they would help here
