Political Science Research Methods — Proposal Part I

Does income inequality reduce voter turnout among low-income citizens in U.S. states, 2000–2020?

Positivist approach U.S. state-level comparison 5 presidential election cycles

Research Question

Does higher income inequality in U.S. states lower voter turnout among citizens in the bottom income quintile across the five presidential elections held between 2000 and 2020?

The question is empirical — it asks about a measurable relationship in the social world, not about what ought to happen. It is scoped to a specific population (low-income citizens), a specific context (U.S. states as units of comparison), and a defined time period (2000–2020). It is answerable as stated using publicly available data: state-level Gini coefficients from the American Community Survey and turnout data disaggregated by income from the Current Population Survey Voting Supplement.

The question is not normative — it does not ask whether inequality should affect participation. It asks whether it measurably does, and through what pathway.

Epistemological Positioning

This study is positioned within a positivist epistemology. Positivism holds that social phenomena exist independently of the researcher and can be observed, measured, and compared across cases. Knowledge is produced by testing falsifiable hypotheses against empirical evidence, and the goal is to identify generalisable patterns — not to interpret the meaning any particular actor attaches to those phenomena.

This stance is justified for this question because both core variables — income inequality and voter turnout — are quantifiable, consistently measured across all fifty states and across multiple election cycles. The relationship between them can be tested causally by controlling for rival explanations (electoral laws, racial composition, urbanisation). The positivist frame also fits the existing literature: nearly all studies of inequality and participation use quantitative, cross-unit comparison — this proposal builds directly onto that tradition rather than departing from it.

A positivist approach would be inappropriate if the question asked how low-income citizens experience or narrate their decision not to vote — that would call for interpretivist methods. But because the question is about aggregate, measurable behaviour across states, positivism is the right fit.

Contribution

Most existing studies test inequality's effect on aggregate turnout — combining all income groups into a single state-level rate. This masks a theoretically important distinction: if inequality primarily demobilises the poor while leaving middle- and upper-income participation stable, aggregate measures will understate the effect.

This study isolates the bottom income quintile as the unit of analysis, using individual-level CPS data aggregated by state. By doing so, it provides a more precise test of the resource-deprivation mechanism: if the effect is real, it should be concentrated in this group. Using state-level panel data across five elections also allows for fixed-effects estimation that controls for time-stable state characteristics — something cross-national studies cannot easily do.

In short: the contribution is methodological precision in testing a well-theorised but often bluntly-measured relationship.

Literature Review

The relationship between economic inequality and political participation sits at the intersection of political economy and democratic theory. Five bodies of work are directly relevant to this proposal.

  • 1

    Verba, Schlozman & Brady — Voice and Equality (1995)

    The foundational account of why citizens participate unequally. Their Civic Voluntarism Model identifies three resources that determine participation: time, money, and civic skills. Low-income citizens are systematically disadvantaged on all three. This model provides the causal mechanism for this proposal — inequality reduces resources, resources predict participation.

  • 2

    Solt — "Economic Inequality and Democratic Political Engagement" (2008)

    The closest empirical antecedent to this study. Solt uses cross-national data to show that greater income inequality is associated with lower political interest, discussion, and voting among the non-wealthy. He finds the demobilising effect is largest for those at the bottom of the income distribution — directly supporting this proposal's focus group.

  • 3

    Leighley & Nagler — Who Votes Now? (2014)

    A detailed account of the income–turnout gap in U.S. elections from 1972 to 2008. Leighley and Nagler document that the gap between high- and low-income turnout has not narrowed over decades, despite rising education levels overall. Their data is essential context for why the 2000–2020 period remains relevant to study.

  • 4

    Lijphart — "Unequal Participation: Democracy's Unresolved Dilemma" (1997)

    Lijphart frames low-income non-voting as a structural democratic problem: when the poor participate less, policy outputs systematically favour the wealthy, which may deepen inequality and further suppress participation — a negative feedback loop. This frames the normative stakes of the empirical question without making the study itself normative.

  • 5

    Meltzer & Richard — "A Rational Theory of the Size of Government" (1981)

    The classical political economy model predicting that higher inequality should increase demand for redistribution among median voters, who gain from it. This proposal uses Meltzer-Richard as a theoretical foil: if their logic held perfectly, inequality should mobilise the poor. The fact that demobilisation is observed instead — as Solt and others show — is what makes this a puzzle worth studying.

Theory and Hypotheses

Theoretical framework

This study draws on the Civic Voluntarism Model (Verba, Schlozman & Brady 1995) as its primary theoretical framework. The model holds that political participation is a function of three individual-level resources: time (free from paid work and caregiving), money (disposable income that can fund participation costs), and civic skills (communication and organisational capacities developed through workplace or civic engagement).

Income inequality — measured at the state level — is theorised to reduce all three resources for citizens in the lowest income quintile. High inequality concentrates income at the top, leaving less disposable income and fewer well-paying jobs (which provide civic skills and schedule flexibility) for those at the bottom. The mechanism is therefore resource deprivation: inequality reduces the material and cognitive inputs that make participation possible.

Hypothesis

H1: U.S. states with higher income inequality (measured by the Gini coefficient) will show lower voter turnout among citizens in the bottom income quintile in presidential elections, controlling for state electoral laws, racial composition, and urbanisation.

Causal model

Independent Variable

State-level Gini coefficient
Source: U.S. Census / American Community Survey — measured at each election year

Causal Mechanism

Resource deprivation: high inequality reduces time, money, and civic skills available to low-income citizens, raising the effective cost of participation

Dependent Variable

Voter turnout rate, bottom income quintile
Source: Current Population Survey Voting Supplement — aggregated to state level

The hypothesis is falsifiable: if states with higher Gini coefficients do not show lower low-income turnout after controls, the resource-deprivation mechanism is not supported. A positive finding would confirm the mechanism; a null finding would point toward alternative explanations, such as political mobilisation efforts that compensate for resource disadvantage.

What became clearer

Working through the IV/DV structure forced me to be precise about what I am actually claiming: not just that "inequality affects voting" but that a specific mechanism (resource deprivation) should produce a specific, testable effect in a specific population. The Meltzer-Richard foil helped me understand why the demobilisation finding is a puzzle — the poor should want to vote for redistribution, which makes their lower turnout interesting rather than obvious. I also understood better why positivism fits: my question is about aggregate behaviour across units, not about how people interpret their own decisions.

What I still find uncertain

I am not yet confident about how to handle the fixed-effects model in practice — I understand the logic (controlling for time-stable state characteristics) but not yet how to run it or interpret the coefficients. I am also uncertain how to deal with reverse causality: does inequality suppress turnout, or does low turnout among the poor allow policies that increase inequality? The panel design helps but does not fully resolve this. I want to understand instrumental variable approaches but haven't got there yet.
